How this directory works

Short version: it does not store a list of contractors. It asks the state for one every time you load the page.

Where the data comes from

The Washington State Department of Labor & Industries publishes its contractor registry as public open data. This site reads three of those datasets directly from your browser:

  • General — business name, license number and type, specialty, status, effective and expiration dates, address, phone, UBI, and principal.
  • Bond — surety company, bond amount, effective and expiration dates.
  • Insurance — carrier, policy number, coverage amount, expiration date.

Those three are joined on the contractor's license number at page load. Because the state serves them with open cross-origin access, no server is needed — which is why this can run as a plain static site while still being current to the day.

What "Licensed · Bonded · Insured" means here

All three badges turn green only when each dataset independently confirms it:

  • Licensed — status is ACTIVE and the registration expiration date has not passed.
  • Bonded — a bond record exists whose expiration is in the future or listed as Until Canceled.
  • Insured — a liability policy exists whose expiration date has not passed.

By default the directory hides anyone missing a current bond or current insurance. You can turn those filters off to see the full registry, including companies whose coverage has lapsed — sometimes useful, but read those listings carefully.

What this site deliberately does not do

It does not rank contractors using state data. The registry contains no reviews, no ratings, and no measure of workmanship — so any site presenting an official-looking "top 100 best contractors" list derived from it is either selling placement or inventing it. The rankings here come from one source only: reviews submitted to this site. That is a far smaller sample than the major platforms, and a contractor missing from those lists is almost certainly just unreviewed here rather than judged and found wanting.

It does not invent certifications. Certification badges only appear for companies whose credentials were confirmed against the certifying body's own directory, with the date and source recorded. Everything else shows no badge — an absent badge means "not verified here," not "not certified." The certification guide links to every official directory so you can check any company yourself in about a minute.

It is not an endorsement. Appearing in this directory means the state has an active registration on file. That is all it means. It is not a recommendation, and it is emphatically not a substitute for references, a written contract, and checking the L&I complaint and bond-claim history on the official record.

How the score is calculated

The scale is modelled on Tabelog, the Japanese restaurant site. Its defining property is not the 1.00–5.00 range but how hard that range is to climb: most businesses sit between 3.0 and 3.5, a 3.5 is genuinely good, and 4.0+ is roughly the top one percent. That compression is the entire point. A scale where everyone scores 4.7 carries no information.

Four mechanisms produce it.

  • Six separate axes. Workmanship, communication, schedule, value, site conduct, and follow-up are rated individually and combined, with workmanship weighted heaviest because it is the most expensive thing to get wrong. Any axis a reviewer skips is excluded rather than scored as zero.
  • Scores are pulled toward the average until they are earned. A single five-star review produces roughly 3.46 — not 5.00. Reaching 4.0 requires both sustained excellence and volume. This is deliberate: it means a handful of reviews from friends, or from a competitor, cannot meaningfully move a score.
  • Ratings are recalibrated against the whole population. Home-services reviews run generous — people rate a contractor they met and shook hands with far more kindly than they would a restaurant. The population is remapped so the typical King County contractor lands near 3.2, which restores meaning to the numbers above it. Both the centre and the spread are rescaled; correcting only the average would squash everyone into an indistinguishable band.
  • Old reviews fade. Weight halves every four years. A review from 2019 may describe a company that no longer employs any of the same people.

Every listing shows the unadjusted average alongside the adjusted score, so the size of the adjustment is always visible rather than hidden behind a black box. Below three reviews a business is marked provisional: still scored, but excluded from all rankings and ineligible for any award.

Awards are relative, not absolute. Gold is the top 1% of rated businesses, Silver the top 5%, Bronze the top 15% — mirroring how the Tabelog Award actually works, as an explicit top-N selection rather than a fixed cutoff. A business scored on its own is never given an award, because an award is a statement about where it stands relative to everyone else.

One limitation worth being blunt about: reviewer credibility weighting is not implemented. Tabelog's main defence against astroturfing is that established reviewers count for more than new accounts — which requires real reviewer identity. Without accounts, that would be theatre. The shrinkage above is what carries the anti-gaming load instead, and it does so by making small numbers of reviews structurally unable to move the needle.

Known limitations, stated plainly

  • Geography is approximate. L&I records a mailing city, not a service area and not a county. This directory filters to King County cities, which means a Tacoma-based roofer who works in Kent every day will not appear, and a Seattle-registered company that only works in Everett will. Filter by trade, then call and ask about service area.
  • Only the primary specialty is matched. The dataset can carry more than one specialty code per contractor; category filtering uses the first. A company registered primarily as GENERAL that also does roofing may appear only under General Contractors.
  • The bond amounts are small. Washington requires $12,000 for general contractors and $6,000 for specialty contractors. Against a real remodel budget that is close to symbolic, and a bond claim is a queue you may share with other homeowners. A voluntarily larger bond is a genuine signal.
  • Registry lag exists. L&I's published extracts refresh periodically rather than instantly, so a very recent lapse or reinstatement may not be reflected. The "Verify on L&I" link on every listing hits the live official record, which is always the tiebreaker.
  • Business names collide constantly. Matching is done on UBI and license number, never on name, precisely because a dozen unrelated companies share variations of the same name.

If the results fail to load

The directory depends on data.wa.gov being reachable from your browser. If the state's service is down or an aggressive ad blocker blocks the request, results will not appear. Nothing is cached locally, by design — a stale cached copy of "who is insured" is exactly the failure mode this site exists to avoid.
