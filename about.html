How this thing works

We do not keep a list of contractors. We ask the state for a fresh one every time you open this page.

Where the data comes from

Washington's Department of Labor & Industries publishes its contractor registry for anyone to read. This site reads three of those files from your browser:

  • General — business name, license number and type, specialty, status, effective and expiration dates, address, phone, UBI, and principal.
  • Bond — surety company, bond amount, effective and expiration dates.
  • Insurance — carrier, policy number, coverage amount, expiration date.

We match the three files by license number when the page loads. There is no server in between and no copy of ours sitting around going stale.

What "Licensed · Bonded · Insured" means here

All three marks turn green only when each file confirms it on its own:

  • Licensed. The status is ACTIVE and the registration has not expired.
  • Bonded. A bond is on file and has not expired.
  • Insured. A liability policy is on file and has not expired.

By default we hide anyone without a current bond or current insurance. You can switch those filters off and see everyone.

What this site will not do

It will not turn state data into a quality ranking. The registry holds no reviews and no ratings. If you see a "top 100 best contractors" list built from it, someone is selling those spots or making them up. Our rankings come from reviews written here and nowhere else. That is a small pool. A contractor missing from a list has probably just not been reviewed here.

It will not invent a credential. A badge appears only after someone confirmed it with the certifying body and wrote down the date and source. No badge means we have not checked. The certification guide links to every official directory so you can check it yourself.

It is not an endorsement. A listing means the state has an active registration on file. Call references. Get a written contract. Read the complaint history on the state's page.

How the score is figured

The scale comes from Tabelog, the Japanese restaurant guide. It is hard to climb on purpose. Most businesses sit between 3.0 and 3.5. A 3.5 is good. A 4.0 is about the top one percent. When everyone scores 4.7 the number stops telling you anything.

Four things keep it honest.

  • Six separate marks. Workmanship, communication, schedule, value, site conduct, and follow-up each get a rating. Workmanship counts the most. Skip a question you cannot answer and we leave it out of the total.
  • Scores start near the middle. One five-star review gives about 3.46. Getting to 4.0 takes good work over many jobs. A few reviews from friends will not move it, and a few from a rival will not either.
  • Everyone is measured against everyone else. People rate contractors generously. We set the typical King County contractor near 3.2 and place the rest around him. We adjust the spread too, so good work still stands out.
  • Old reviews fade. Weight halves every four years. The crew from 2019 may be gone.

We show the raw average next to the adjusted one so you can see how far we moved it. Under three reviews a business is marked thin. We still score it, but we keep it off the rankings and it wins no awards.

Awards go to the top of the field. Gold is the top 1 percent of rated businesses, Silver the top 5, Bronze the top 15. This is how the Tabelog Award works. A business scored on its own never gets one.

One thing we have not built: reviewer credibility weighting. Tabelog counts long-standing reviewers more than new accounts. That needs real identities. Without them it would only look like a safeguard. The pull toward the middle does that job instead.

Where this falls short

  • The geography is rough. L&I records a mailing address, not a service area. A roofer in Tacoma who works in Kent every week will not show up here. A Seattle company that only works in Everett will. Call and ask where they work.
  • One trade per contractor. The state's published file carries a single specialty per registration, even though plenty of contractors work in more than one trade. A company filed as GENERAL that also roofs will only ever appear under General Contractors. Check a few related trades, and ask what else they do.
  • The bonds are small. $30,000 for a general contractor, $15,000 for a specialty contractor. Those amounts rose on July 1, 2024, from $12,000 and $6,000, where they had sat since 2002. Even so, other homeowners may be ahead of you in line to collect. Work out what it covers on your job with the bond calculator.
  • A blank bond is not proof of no bond. Roughly 46% of active registrations have no row at all in the published bond file. That share is flat for every year back to 2010. Real lapses would move around by year, so this describes the file and not the contractors. Washington also lets a contractor file an assigned savings account in place of a surety bond. That would not appear here either. When we show no bond, it means the state publishes none. Ask them for proof.
  • The state's file is not instant. L&I republishes on its own schedule. A change from this week may not show yet. Every listing links to the live record. If the two disagree, the state is right.
  • Names collide. We match on UBI and license number, never on the name. Dozens of unrelated companies here share versions of the same one.

If nothing loads

This page needs data.wa.gov. If the state's service is down, or an ad blocker gets in the way, you will see nothing. We keep no copy on hand. A saved answer about who is insured goes stale quietly.
